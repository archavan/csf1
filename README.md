**Title**: Protein aggregate removal by macrophages via trogocytosis

**Authors**: Yoshitaka Kimura, Kazuki Sato, Arun R. Chavan, William Kasberg, Malan Silva, Wei Qiu, Nirmala Iyer, Michael Innerberger, Igor Siwanowicz, Scott D. Pope, Athreya Rangavajhula, Gang-Qing Yao, Karl Insogna, Eric T. Trautman, Christopher K. E. Bleck, Xiaolei Su, Jennifer Lippincott-Schwartz, Ruslan Medzhitov

**Abstract**: Protein aggregate formation can cause cell damage and contribute to tissue pathologies including neurodegeneration.  Protein aggregates are normally eliminated by cell-intrinsic mechanisms of lysosomal (autophagic) degradation.  However, degradative capacity varies between cell types, raising the possibility that high-capacity cell types, such as macrophages, may contribute to aggregate disposal from the low-capacity cells.  Here, we show that cellular protein aggregates are removed by macrophages via trogocytosis — a partial engulfment of the target cells. Lysosomal stress upon protein aggregate accumulation promotes cell surface expression of the transmembrane isoform of the macrophage growth factor CSF1 (tmCSF1), which is detected by macrophages through the CSF1 receptor to drive trogocytosis. Insufficient or impaired degradation of protein aggregates in lysosomes results in their transport along microtubules to the plasma membrane where they physically associate with tmCSF1. This enables macrophages to specifically trogocytose protein aggregates. These findings reveal a protein homeostasis system that eliminates protein aggregates in a non-cell-autonomous manner.
